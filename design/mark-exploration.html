Four marks, and why three of them fail

The old mark was a picture of a weather vane — it named the category and said nothing about the product. These four try to encode something true instead: that Vane resolves a noisy field into one reading, and flags the thing that doesn't fit. Every one is shown at 16px, in one colour, and on black, because that's where marks actually die.

A · Barb

Recommended

A wind barb — the notation meteorologists use to write down speed and direction in one glyph. Not a picture of an instrument, a reading taken by one. Each barb is a slot; lengths vary because activity varies; one barb is longer, brass, and crosses the staff — the anomaly that broke the pattern. That's the product in four strokes, and the vertical staff means it collides with no common UI icon.

B · Sounding

Fails at 16px

The scope's own signature: slot planes receding into depth, one of them flat and flagged. Conceptually the strongest of the four — it's a literal miniature of the thing that makes the product distinctive. And it dies small. Stacked horizontal lines at favicon size are indistinguishable from a hamburger menu, and you'd spend forever being mistaken for a nav control. Worth keeping as a section device or a loading state; not as the mark.

C · Bracket

Reads as “more”

The sandwich signature abstracted: two outer marks, one displaced victim between them, enclosed. Sharp idea, sharp shape — but three dots in a row is the universal glyph for an overflow menu. At small sizes people will try to click it. It also welds the brand to one detector, when the whole point is that more are coming.

D · Resolve

Beautiful, unusable

Scattered strokes on the left settling into a single vector on the right — noise becoming direction, which is the most accurate description of what Vane does. It's the best idea here and the worst mark: the whole meaning lives in the difference between the scattered end and the resolved end, and by 16px that difference is two pixels. Keep it. Use it as the loading animation while an analysis runs, where it gets to be big and move.

Vane

Why the brass barb matters. Take it out and you have a tidy little chart glyph. Leave it in and the mark states the product's thesis before anyone reads a word: most of what happens is ordinary, one thing isn't, and we point at that one. It's also the only element that survives being shrunk — at 16px the mark reduces to a staff, two ticks, and the odd one out, which is still the whole idea.
